What if your next lesson started with a question, instead of a wall of text? Here is a solar-system overview. We can look at the whole picture, choose Earth, explore a detail, and return without losing our place. That is the presentation we are going to build in TrySlides. We will start with a blank slide, create a clear visual structure, and turn that structure into a small interactive learning experience. This is a handmade example, not an automatic lesson generator. By the end, you will know how to adapt the same approach to a topic you teach.

Before opening the editor, decide what the learner should understand. Our goal is deliberately small: recognize the overview, locate Earth, and explore a focused explanation. We are not trying to teach the entire solar system in one screen. We are designing a starting point for a conversation. The learning question is: where is Earth in our solar system? Our presentation needs three moments: an overview, an Earth detail, and a clear route back. Think of those moments as a path, not a collection of effects. The images are illustrations, not scientific photographs, and this is a simplified diagram. Sizes and distances are not to scale. The layout helps us discuss the planets; it does not model their orbits or real proportions. Keep that distinction clear while you build. Every extra label should help answer the question rather than create another topic to explain.

Start in the desktop editor and create a new blank presentation. Name it Our Solar System. We are starting from scratch because seeing the structure take shape is useful for this lesson. This is a manually built example, not a ready-made solar-system template. The slide canvas is where the content lives. The slide navigation keeps track of the presentation, and the properties panel gives us controls for the selected element. Those are the areas we need first. Add the heading Our Solar System, then place a shorter question underneath: Where is Earth? The heading identifies the subject; the question gives the viewer something to find. Keep both inside the slide boundaries. Now choose the Dark presentation theme. The slide becomes black, with contrasting light text. This changes the presentation theme, not just the editor appearance. The dark backdrop is a choice for this lesson, not a requirement for every presentation. We will check the finished composition in presentation mode later.

Now let us build the overview with transparent images and editable labels. Bring in the Sun and the eight planet images, then adjust their positions and sizes on the dark canvas. Keep each image in proportion, especially Saturn with its wider rings. We are arranging a teaching illustration, not drawing a scale model. Follow the sequence outward from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. In this composition, the arrangement curves around the available space instead of using a numbered grid. Earth sits between Venus and Mars. Next, add the planet names as separate text elements. Position each name close enough to identify its image, without hiding the surface or overlapping another label. The names matter because color alone is not a reliable instruction. A learner should not have to guess which blue object we mean. The image placement and labeling were recorded separately; together they build this overview. Repeated placement and typing can be shortened, while the useful actions remain clear. Before continuing, look at the whole diagram. Can you identify Earth from its label and its neighbors? Keep in mind that neither the displayed sizes nor the gaps represent real astronomical scale.

A diagram becomes useful when the viewer knows where to look. Let us read the hierarchy in this overview. The heading names the topic. The shorter question gives the learner a task. The planet labels identify the images, while their order helps us locate Earth. Each piece of text has a different job. Notice the space between the objects and their labels: the words should remain connected to the right planet without competing with the image. Notice how the title, question, images, and labels share the work of explaining the scene. The dark background supports the chosen tone, but the content still needs to be readable. Check the title, question, and labels at a normal viewing size. If anything is hard to read, adjust it before continuing. The goal is not to add more decoration. It is to make the next action clear.

This is where Steps become useful. Base is the starting visual state of our slide: the full overview. A Step is another state of that same slide. It lets us focus on part of the explanation without building a separate slide from scratch. Create a Step from the controls above the canvas. In this recording, the new state is called Step 1. It will be our Earth detail. Keep the Earth image that is already in the overview. Move the Sun, the other planet images, and their labels outside the slide area in this Step. Objects outside the black slide are still visible around the editor canvas; do not confuse that editing workspace with the presentation frame. Now enlarge the existing Earth and position it on the right, leaving room for explanation on the left. Do not duplicate it or import another Earth just to make the detail. We want the same element to carry the change of focus. The overview title and question are still present at this intermediate stage; we will change the detail copy next. Switch between the overview and Step 1 to compare their roles. The overview provides context; the detail provides focus. One slide can serve both purposes through its different visual states.

In Step 1, the text should now explain Earth rather than introduce the entire solar system. Change the detail heading to Earth. Use two lines underneath: Third planet from the Sun. Find Earth in the overview. The first line answers the question from the starting view. The second line gives the learner a small task when they return. In this recording, the existing title and description are edited for the detail state. Keep the overview text intact in Base, and check that the two states still say what they should. The enlarged Earth stays on the right, with the explanation on the left. We do not need to fill the space with more facts just because there is room. Add a separate text element labeled Back below the explanation. At this point it is only visible content; we still need to configure what happens when someone clicks it. A button-shaped object, or a text label that looks like a control, does not become navigation on its own. Next we will connect Earth and Back to their destinations.

Return to the overview and select the Earth image. In Properties, use the plus button beside Interactions. Choose Click as the event and set the destination to Step 1, then save the interaction. The saved Earth interaction identifies its action as Go to Step 1. That gives us the outward route. The Step defines what we see when we arrive; the interaction defines how we get there. Those are different jobs. Now switch to Step 1 and select the Back text. Give it a Click interaction that returns to Base, and save it. Configure the return on the Back element in the detail state, not on an unrelated element in the overview. Interactions belong to their state, so check both directions rather than assuming that one setting completes the whole journey. Our example uses clicks, with a visible return control. We are not relying on hover as the only way to explore. Once the settings are saved, test the route in presentation mode. Selecting objects in the editor is not the same as following their interactions during playback.

Use Present to test the route as your audience will experience it. First, pause on the overview. Can you read the heading and planet labels without leaning toward the screen? Click Earth and let the focused state finish moving. Read the explanation, then use Back. Repeat the journey once more. We are checking more than whether a click works. We are checking whether the viewer understands where they are and how to return. Look for content clipped by the presentation frame, labels covered by larger objects, or a return control that disappears into the background. If something fails, go back to the correct state in the editor, fix the actual destination or layout, and test again. Keep one uninterrupted recording of the successful round trip. That recording is our evidence that this path works; an editorial transition is not a substitute for it.

The delivery format changes what the audience can do. Open the presentation menu and look under Downloads. Editable gives you a tryslides file: a portable copy you can keep as a backup or send to someone else. They can import it into TrySlides and edit their own copy. That is a file hand-off, not live collaboration. Offline is the HTML option for browser playback with Steps and interactions. PDF gives you static pages for the slide and its Steps, without the interactive navigation or animation. Plain text gives you the written content without the images or visual layout. Choose the format for what your learner needs. Before sending a file, open and check it: test the route in HTML, inspect the PDF pages, or verify the editable copy imports correctly. None of these options is a video export. The tutorial you are watching is edited separately.

Let us separate the method from the solar-system artwork. We started with one question. We built an overview that made the question visible. We used a focused state for the explanation, added a clear route back, and tested the full journey. You can apply that structure to a labeled diagram, a map of ideas, or the parts of a process. The content changes; the need for clear orientation does not. A useful next exercise is to build a second detail state for a different planet, with its own verified fact and its own return interaction. We have not built that extension in this lesson, so it is a practice task rather than a hidden feature of our finished example. Start small, keep the labels readable, and let each state earn its place in the explanation.

You now have a complete path: a clear overview, a focused explanation, and a way back. That is the foundation of this small interactive lesson in TrySlides. For your own version, choose one topic and one question. Build the simplest overview that supports it, add one useful detail, and test the route before you add anything else.
