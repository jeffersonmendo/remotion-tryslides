# Audiovisual direction and editing

## Scope and creative direction

This is the shared editorial direction for **all TrySlides videos**: commercials, feature announcements, product demonstrations, explainers, tutorials, and social clips. It guides planning, writing, recording requests, scene design, animation, sound, editing, and revisions. It is not limited to tutorials or to writing scripts.

**Owner-requested direction:** make videos visually varied and engaging to listen to, with intentional scenes, fluent narration where appropriate, synchronized representations of the message, and purposeful music and sound effects. Screen recording is one storytelling resource, not the permanent background of every video.

The execution guidance below translates that direction into production recommendations. It does not establish new product capabilities or override the [visual identity](visual-identity.md) and [truthful-claims reference](limits-and-claims.md).

## Central principle: represent the message

Every meaningful beat needs an intentional visual treatment. Voice, music, effects, images, typography, and movement should express the same idea, complement one another, or deliberately set up a reveal.

Do not leave the home screen visible merely because a recording is available while the narration explains something unrelated. Conversely, do not force a new full-screen scene for every noun or word. A new image, highlight, camera framing, or transformation within the current scene may be enough.

Choose what best explains the current thought:

| Message or intention                   | Possible visual treatment                                | Synchronization anchor                                |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Name a relevant subject, such as a dog | Show a useful image of that subject                      | Reveal it when the subject is named                   |
| Enumerate colors, benefits, or steps   | Reveal the items progressively in one composition        | Each item appears as it is named                      |
| Compare two options                    | Place them side by side with meaningful labels           | Highlight the option being discussed                  |
| Explain a relationship or change       | Use a diagram or a simple transformation                 | Connect or transform at the corresponding explanation |
| Demonstrate a real product action      | Show the actual interface and action                     | The instruction and visible action remain aligned     |
| Introduce a chapter or a new intention | Use a brief title scene or a clear change in composition | The heading arrives with the spoken introduction      |
| Reveal the outcome                     | Show the finished presentation or concrete result        | Hold after the reveal so it can be understood         |
| Introduce a musical turn               | Change composition, reveal a result, or transition       | Use a relevant musical accent, not every beat         |

Literal illustration is not always the best choice. Showing a dog helps when the example concerns a dog; illustrating an incidental metaphor may distract from the real point. Meaning takes priority over word matching.

## Scene vocabulary

Combine the following as needed, without imposing fixed proportions or a scene change every few seconds:

- **Product footage:** the real workspace, editor, playback, or output. Use it for actions and verifiable product results.
- **Animated typography:** short phrases arriving in words or meaningful groups. Use it for introductions, emphasis, questions, and transitions, not as a substitute for every visual explanation.
- **Concept scene:** a clean background with diagrams, objects, images, or relationships that explain an idea away from the interface.
- **Reference board:** selected examples, a palette, and visual references that communicate the direction of a presentation or campaign.
- **List or comparison:** progressive items, color cards, alternatives, or before/after views.
- **Result scene:** the finished artifact, playback, or a comparison that makes the outcome visible.
- **Chapter scene:** a short section heading that helps the audience understand the next stage.
- **Closing scene:** a concise message and a real, appropriate CTA.

Captions reproduce speech accessibly. Animated headings and emphasis text organize or emphasize ideas. Keep those roles distinct; avoid filling the screen with two competing versions of the narration.

## Framing and scene changes

A framing change within the same recording is not necessarily a new scene. Zoom to a control when attention needs to move there; return to context when location or the resulting composition matters. Do not zoom merely because a click occurred.

Use a full scene change when the purpose changes: action to explanation, overview to detail, one chapter to another, or setup to payoff. Use a direct cut for continuity, a visual match when the same object carries the story, and a designed transition when a clear change of context benefits from it. Avoid making every transition a spectacle.

For each change, decide:

1. What should the audience look at next?
2. What connects the outgoing and incoming scenes?
3. What voice phrase, action, or sound motivates the change?
4. What must remain visible long enough to be understood?

Color changes can separate chapters or kinds of explanation, but white/black alternation is not mandatory. Use the visual reference and check contrast and consistency. A sample presentation's palette is not a new global brand palette.

## Narration: fluent, detailed, and edited

For narrated explainers and tutorials, favor useful, sustained narration over long accidental gaps. Explain the objective, the relevant action, why it matters, and how to recognize the result. Detail should add understanding, not describe every cursor movement or repeat the same point.

Record calmly, then edit:

1. Select the intended takes and remove errors, abandoned phrases, and unnecessary repetition.
2. Shorten long accidental gaps and waiting time between useful phrases.
3. Preserve word endings, natural breaths, sentence boundaries, and deliberate pauses.
4. Listen across each join for clipped speech, abrupt room-tone changes, or a rushed rhythm; adjust the cut rather than applying one silence threshold blindly.
5. Use the edited narration as a timing backbone for visual beats and captions, adjusting it when the demonstration needs more explanation or observation time.

No dead time does **not** mean zero silence. Mark a deliberate reading, reveal, or dramatic pause in the storyboard so it has a purpose. A music-led commercial may intentionally have little or no voiceover; do not force the tutorial narration pattern onto every format.

If speech and product actions were recorded together, preserve their synchronization when trimming. Do not close audio gaps while leaving the associated action at its old time. For separate voiceover, edit the footage to the approved narration without inventing product behavior.

Speed up repetitive entry or waiting where appropriate; do not accelerate the essential operation the viewer needs to follow. Shortened processing should not imply a measured speed guarantee.

## Music and sound design

Plan three separate roles:

- **Voice:** carries the explanation or message when present.
- **Music:** supports tone, continuity, buildup, contrast, and resolution.
- **Effects:** reinforce selected actions and transitions, such as clicks, drags, reveals, pauses, and scene exits.

Keep speech intelligible. Reduce music or effects under important words and allow musical changes at intentional boundaries. There is no required sound on every click, drag, word, or cut. Excessive effects compete with the message.

Editorial effects may reinforce the video without being real application sounds. Never imply that added click, drag, or transition effects are built-in TrySlides audio features. Use licensed or owned assets and record their provenance in the brief.

Audio can connect scenes: the next explanation or music can begin before the next image appears, or the previous phrase can continue over a new visual. Keep the timing clear and do not speak about a control that has disappeared before the viewer can locate it.

### Example: “But first, let's pause”

An editorial cursor clicks a pause symbol, the demonstration image freezes, and the music softens while the voice continues with an explanation. A deliberate visual and sonic exit then returns to the product footage.

This is a storytelling device, not evidence that the TrySlides editor contains that pause feature. Make the editorial frame distinct from the real interface. Choose whether the music pauses, fades, or continues softly based on the message; do not stop all sound automatically.

## Example sequence: planning and creating a presentation

This illustrative structure is not a required template or a claim about a completed video. Use the actual language chosen in the brief.

| Voice or cue                             | Visual representation                                                             | Editing / sound intention                                            |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Let's create a presentation”            | Brief animated heading on a clean light background                                | Establish the new chapter without a long logo intro                  |
| “We'll use green, blue, red, and yellow” | A contrasting palette scene; each color, name, and chosen HEX appears as named    | Keep the completed list visible; optional restrained reveal accents  |
| “For children, with a minimalist style”  | A reference board with appropriate images, simple layouts, and the chosen palette | Explain the design direction instead of showing an idle workspace    |
| “We'll call it ‘What are cows?’”         | Actual footage entering the name and creating the presentation                    | Match voice and actions; show the confirmation/result                |
| “Before we start, here's the editor”     | A brief chapter transition, then an orientation view of the editor                | Restore context before close views of relevant regions               |
| Explanation of a Step                    | A simple conceptual visual, followed by real playback proving the behavior        | Distinguish the explanatory animation from the product demonstration |

The colors and child-oriented presentation are example creative choices, not TrySlides brand requirements. Do not invent HEX values as existing brand facts or imply this is a catalog template.

## Adapt the treatment to the video

| Video type            | Primary job                                            | Editorial emphasis                                                                      |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Commercial / campaign | Make a specific value memorable and motivate an action | A focused premise, intentional audio rhythm, selective product proof, and a clear close |
| Feature announcement  | Communicate what is available and why it matters       | Benefit, credible demonstration, and an understandable result                           |
| Product demonstration | Show an actual workflow and outcome                    | Clear product footage alternating with helpful context and explanation                  |
| Explainer             | Make a concept understandable                          | Diagrams, examples, comparisons, and synchronized narration                             |
| Tutorial              | Help the viewer reproduce a result                     | Chapter structure, visible essential actions, explanatory narration, and checkpoints    |
| Social cutdown        | Communicate one useful idea in a smaller format        | Reframe deliberately; retain the context needed to support the claim                    |

These are flexible starting points. Do not turn a long tutorial into a rapid commercial montage, or force every commercial into a step-by-step walkthrough.

## Plan the audiovisual edit before polishing

Use [the video brief](video-brief.md) to identify every beat's message, scene type, voice or music cue, visual action, entry/exit, and required material. Request only the recordings needed for those scenes; also plan the images, diagrams, typography, music, and effects that will be produced or supplied.

Develop a rough sequence with draft narration before perfecting every animation. After the voice edit, refine synchronization and transitions, then add music, selected effects, captions, and final visual polish. Revisit timing when the actual footage differs from the plan.

Keep the original recording or recording-app project. If an export already contains zooms or effects, note them before adding more in Remotion. A flattened cropped shot cannot recover the image outside its frame. Do not assume cursor metadata accompanies an ordinary video file or automatic tracking is configured in this project.

## Editorial review

- Every meaningful beat has a relevant visual treatment, not unrelated filler footage.
- Named list items, reveals, and essential actions align with their audio cues.
- Full scene changes follow a change in intention; within-scene emphasis is used when sufficient.
- Product actions and outcomes remain truthful and understandable after cuts or speed changes.
- Narration is fluent without accidental waiting, clipped speech, or relentless pacing.
- Deliberate pauses serve comprehension or the story; they are not treated as defects automatically.
- Music and selected effects support the scene without masking speech or pretending to be product features.
- Titles, captions, palette labels, and UI have enough viewing time and do not compete for attention.
- The piece works as a coherent audiovisual story, not merely a collection of animated components.

Use these checks alongside the factual and delivery checks in [video production](video-production.md). Motion, constant speech, and frequent cuts alone do not guarantee an engaging video.
